It’s strange, the things you overhear when you’re no longer listening for them. In my younger years, my ears pricked at every murmur in a tavern, every hissed conversation in a shadowed alley. Paranoia kept me alive through more scrapes than I care to count. These days, retired (mostly) in Silverymoon’s moonstone-lit libraries or advising guilds on monster taxonomy, I let the noise wash over me like river fog. But some echoes refuse to fade—especially those from Thay.  

I hadn’t meant to go back. Thay, that scarlet-stained nightmare of necromantic ambition and slave markets, had left its scars on me decades prior—a superficial claw mark from a ghoul here, a lingering distrust of overly polite wizards there. But when a colleague at the Conservatory needed rare components from the Okothian Fungi Groves north of Eltabbar, and no sellsword would brave the necropolis-infested trade routes, old habits tugged at my boots. “One last errand,” I’d told myself, lying through my teeth.  

The Azure Veil tavern in Eltabbar was supposed to be neutral ground—a place where Red Wizards, foreign merchants, and the occasional fool (like myself) could share bad wine and worse company without drawing daggers. They’d draped the walls in illusory waterfalls to muffle sound, a clever trick that painted the air with shimmering turquoise light. I was nursing a tankard of something sour, mentally tallying fungal spore prices, when the enchantment flickered.  

Just for a moment. A glitch in the spellwork near a corner booth. And through it, like frost cracking glass, slithered a voice: *“—the Tomb-Rot has spread to the eleventh catacomb. Szass Tam’s lackeys haven’t noticed.”*  

Thayans love their euphemisms. “Tomb-Rot” could mean anything from actual decay to a coup plot. But the second voice tightened my grip on my tankard: *“Good. The Phylactery Vault must be cleansed before the Ascendant Night. Only then will the Bloodless Emperor rise.”*  

You grow old in this business when you learn the weight of certain phrases. *Bloodless Emperor* wasn’t a term thrown around in polite Thayan society. It reeked of old cults, the kind even Szass Tam’s lich-regime stomped out for being “excessive.” And the Ascendant Night? That was Thay’s oldest holiday—a night when the dead were said to walk without a necromancer’s leash. A night, legends whispered, when ancient things buried beneath the red soil stirred.  

The speakers were two men—human, likely. Hooded, though Thayan fashion leaned toward drama, so that meant little. But their hands… the first speaker gestured while picking at a plate of pickled kraken, and the candlelight caught the skin of his wrist: mottled grey, with a fine network of cracks, like pottery glaze. The other’s fingers, drumming the table, were too still. No rise and fall of breath.  

*Undead*. But not mindless husks. Sentient. *Ambitious*.  

I kept my head down, the tankard hiding half my face, and let my old rogue’s reflexes take over. Ear angled toward the flickering illusion, fingers casually loosening the strap on my boot dagger (enchanted, silvered, and a gift from a grateful Harper—never leave home without it). Their conversation dropped lower, but I caught shards:  

**First Cultist**: “—sacrifices are ready. Twelve slaves from the Mulan plantations. Their fear will nourish the ritual.”  
**Second Cultist**: “The seal on the vault is… resilient. The Rosethorn Guild’s cipher blades failed.”  
**First Cultist**: “Then we invoke *Her*. The One Who Walks Beneath Dreams. She demands a living key.”  

A pause. The clink of a goblet.  

**Second Cultist**: “The Harper agent in Bezantur. She’s close to uncovering our operatives. Deal with her.”  
**First Cultist**: “Already done. Her blood will anoint the gates.”  

Ice coiled in my gut. I hadn’t come to Thay for heroics—just mushrooms and money. But hearing “Harper” and “her blood” in the same breath had that old fire sparking. Before I could weigh sense against sentiment (leaving the latter, as usual, the victor), an emaciated server shuffled past their booth. The illusion spell repaired itself, drowning their words back into waterfall murmur.  

But I’d heard enough. The Phylactery Vault—an actual place, a hidden repository rumored to hold the soul-jars of Thay’s long-dead Sorcerer-Kings. The Ascendant Night, six days hence. And a cult intending to crack the vault open to resurrect a “Bloodless Emperor” (old texts I’d skimmed years ago suggested that title belonged to Thay’s first lich, a tyrant named Nheras who’d devoured souls instead of binding them).  

This wasn’t just sacrilege; it was catastrophic. If Nheras’s spirit broke free, even Szass Tam might struggle to contain it. Worse, modern Thay’s undead rulers, for all their evils, kept the region’s nightmares in check. A free-roaming demilich emperor could turn the entire nation into a necrotic wasteland—one that might spill into the neighboring Moonsea or the Ride.  

So, Alim Kors did what he does best: the ridiculously inadvisable.

**The Thay I Remember—and the One That Remembers Me**  

Tracking undead cultists isn’t like trailing bandits. They don’t sleep, eat, or leave the usual trash. But they *do* leave trails of the unnatural. Cold spots. A scent of loam and iron. Crow carcasses with their eyes pecked out—a calling card I recognized from a case years back involving a death cult in the Cloakwood.  

I followed them to the Ribcage District—a slum outside Eltabbar’s walls where the air tasted of ash and the wails of slaves being branded. The cultists slipped into a bone-white tower camouflaged as a derelict granary. Magical locks shimmered on the door, but my old Harper contacts had left me with tricks: a vial of phase-spider venom mixed with quicksilver. One drop on the lock, and the wards pulsed sickly green before dissolving.  

Inside, the tower was a hive of quiet horror. Zombie laborers chained to pulpits transcribed scrolls in their own blood. Chained quasits shrieked prophecies in Abyssal. And in a central chamber, hanging from chains, was a woman I recognized—Lyra Velsin, a Harper agent I’d crossed paths with in Neverwinter. Alive, though bruised and bleeding from a gash at her temple. The “living key,” no doubt.  

The cultists—six now, not two—stood chanting around a pit filled with liquid shadow. Above it hovered a *thing* sculpted from lightless stone—a skeletal figure with antlers of blackened bone and eyes like dying stars. Nheras’s idol, I presumed. The One Who Walks Beneath Dreams.  

“We greet thee, Mother of Tombs,” droned the lead cultist, the one with the cracked grey skin. “Accept this key, and bless our blades with entropy!”  

This was it—no time for stealth. I kicked open the door, dagger in one hand, a flask of alchemist’s fire in the other.  

“Evening, gents. Heard you were redecorating. Mind if I critique the aesthetic?”  

Chaos, blessedly, erupted. Zombies lurched toward me. The cultists screeched in fury. I flung the flask into the shadow-pit. It ignited with a whoosh, blue flames licking the idol’s feet. Nheras’s statue *screamed*, a sound that rattled my teeth. The distraction let me cut Lyra down—she was woozy but coherent enough to snatch a fallen cultist’s dagger.  

What followed was less duel and more demolition derby. Lyra and I fought back-to-back, blades against claws and spells. She shattered a necromantic focus with a well-thrown dagger; I jammed my silvered blade into the lead cultist’s chest—only for it to *stick*. His grey flesh writhed, healing around the metal.  

“Flesh golem shell!” Lyra shouted. “The real body’s inside!”  

Ah. *That’s* why his skin looked like cracked pottery. I wrenched my dagger free and went for the neck—a golem’s weakest joint. The head toppled, revealing not bone, but a shriveled, mummified human head grafted within. The cultist howled—a wet, gurgling sound—as the body crumpled.  

The rest fell quickly. One fled into the shadows and, judging by the sudden cessation of screaming, met something hungrier than us. When silence fell, we stood panting amid scorch marks and twitching limbs. The idol lay in pieces, its dark aura snuffed.  

Lyra gripped my forearm. “Kors? Gods, I thought you were dead.”  

“Retired,” I corrected, wiping golem ichor off my blade. “What in the Nine Hells were you after?”  

Her face darkened. “They’d bribed officials in Bezantur to smuggle relics—fragments of Nheras’s bone-codex. With those and a living sacrifice, they could’ve unsealed his vault. You stopped a second Sundering.”  

I nodded, then eyed the pit. The flames had died, leaving char and a lingering stench. “They mentioned calling on ‘Her.’ Nheras’s consort?”  

“Nyraasha. A lesser death goddess, imprisoned centuries ago.” She nudged a cultist’s robe with her boot, revealing a tattoo of a skeletal serpent coiled around a black sun. “Her cult’s been quiet since the Zulkirs purged their temples. Guess ‘quiet’ isn’t ‘gone.’”

**Moonlight and Memory**  

We burned the tower. Let Thay’s authorities assume it was rival necromancers. Lyra vanished into the night with promises to “file a report”—Harper jargon for “I’ll downplay your involvement.” I delivered the fungi spores, collected my coin, and rode north, the stink of smoke clinging to my cloak.  

You’d think such a tale would end there. But Thay doesn’t let go lightly. Even now, years later in Silverymoon’s cozy guildhalls, I dream of that idol—the unbearable *weight* in its gaze. Sometimes, when I stroll the Moonbridge at twilight, I swear the shadows ripple a little too hungrily.  

And last Midsummer, a package arrived at the Conservatory. No return address. Inside: a cracked grey pottery shard and a scroll with three words in spidery Thayan script:  

*“He remembers you.”*  

So much for retirement.  

I keep the shard locked in a lead-lined box. Let Nheras whisper. I’ve faced worse. And if the shadows ever come calling again? Well.  

Silverymoon’s libraries hold more than books—they hold artifacts. And old adventurers? We hold grudges.
